Up, down, and out

At Babel, people tried to climb up to heaven with one language and one plan. God's answer was to come down: first in a vision of a ladder, then in his Son, then in his Spirit at Pentecost.

What went wrong at BabelGenesis 11

God had told Noah's family to fill the earth. Babel's builders wanted the opposite: to stay together “lest we be scattered.” Their tower was a monument to self-sufficiency, and their shared language made the project seem unstoppable. God confused the language that had made the plan possible.

  • The goal: a name for themselves.
  • The tool: one unified language.
  • The judgment: language divided, people scattered.

Babylon through the ages

Babel becomes Babylon, and Babylon becomes a symbol that runs all the way to Revelation.

Nimrod: the prototypeGenesis 10

Nimrod was “a mighty hunter before the LORD,” and “the beginning of his kingdom was Babel.” Genesis doesn't say he built the tower, but it ties him to the place, and he founds Nineveh too: Israel's two great enemy capitals start with him.

Achan: Babylon gets into the campJoshua 7

Among the spoil of Jericho, Achan saw a Babylonish garment, silver and gold. He coveted, took and hid them, and Israel lost at Ai. The pull of Babylon was already working inside God's people.

Nebuchadnezzar: “Is not this great Babylon, that I have built?”Daniel

The king set up a golden image and commanded every people and language to bow. Later he boasted of his own power and was humbled until he acknowledged that the Most High rules. Three young men refused to bow, and God was with them in the fire.

The woman in the basketZechariah 5

Zechariah sees wickedness shut in an ephah, the standard measure of trade, and carried to the land of Shinar to have a house built for it. Babylon's return is pictured as commerce and idolatry together.

Babylon the greatRevelation

Revelation's Babylon controls buying and selling, demands worship, and trades in everything up to human souls. Then it falls “in one hour,” and God's people are told: “Come out of her, my people.”

God's answer: the ladder and the Spirit

The Spirit and the algorithm

Large language models can translate between nearly every tongue, a kind of reversed Babel built by human hands. They can be useful tools. The danger is trusting them for what only God gives .

GuiltThe inner life

A chatbot tends to soothe: everyone makes mistakes. That may ease the feeling, but it leaves the guilt. The Spirit convicts, which leads to repentance, forgiveness and a conscience truly made clean.

AnxietyThe inner life

Techniques and reassurance can help manage symptoms. The peace of God “passeth all understanding” and keeps the heart and mind in Christ, whatever the circumstances.

DecisionsThe outer life

A decision matrix recommends the safest or most profitable path. God gives wisdom to those who ask, and sometimes calls people, like Abraham, to go out “not knowing whither he went.”

LonelinessThe outer life

Simulated companionship asks nothing of us: no patience, no sacrifice, no risk. The Spirit assures us we are God's children and sends us into a real church, where love is costly, messy and genuine.

Practical guard railsDiscernment
  • Use tools, but don't hand moral, theological or pastoral decisions to an algorithm.
  • Test everything, including what a machine tells you about Scripture, against Scripture itself.
  • Keep prayer, confession and fellowship personal and human.

For discussion

  1. Babel's builders wanted to avoid being scattered . What makes people today want one system, one platform, one voice?
  2. Achan saw, coveted, took and hid . Where do you see that sequence in how technology pulls at us?
  3. Jacob's ladder came down to him while he slept . What does that say about how we come to God?
  4. At Pentecost each heard in his own language . Why does it matter that the Spirit didn't make everyone speak one tongue?
  5. Which of the Spirit's works (comfort, conviction, discernment, intercession) are you most tempted to look for somewhere else?
